1917
The Origin
1910s – 1920s

Early Japanese Animation Begins

Japanese animation began developing in the early 20th century. Japanese anime "really took off in the late 1910s and 1920s" with the true date of birth being in 1917. This 'start' was "when Japanese artists were testing animated films."

The common works that we see in today's shows are different from what it was when it was first being produced. The early works were short and often westernized cartoons. This could be seen as the birth of anime, which would soon become a global phenomenon.

Wartime Era
1930s – 1940s

Animation Ties to National Wartime

During the 1930s and 1940s, Japanese animation became more organized through studios. Studios produced anime which served as entertainment, education, and propaganda. This change in production occurred mainly due to WW2.

Because of the big changes that followed the aftermath of the war, "Japan was rebuilding everything, including its anime industries." In comparison to the previous era, you "start to see action, more emotion, and the anime stories that continue over time instead of just one-off anime pieces." WW2 intrinsically connected artists to the warfront, increasing production of the medium. Overall, anime during this time became tools for communicating stories to a broader audience.

Postwar Era
1950s

Postwar Rebuilding

After World War II, Japan rebuilt its media industries. Studios such as Toei Doga aimed to produce higher-quality animated films that would compete with the popular Western Cartoons. For years after the war, anime was produced but animation from abroad — mostly American Cartoons — was more profitable and popular in Japan.

This is highly because most of the anime in Japan was considered to be "advertising or propaganda." Although anime was struggling to gain popularity, during this period Manga was growing rapidly. The large growth of Manga set up many strong storylines for anime to be produced off of.

The Tezuka Revolution
1963

Astro Boy

Osamu Tezuka's Astro Boy was arguably the biggest turning point in anime history. Tezuka is often considered "The god of manga." He was the first to take his manga and "[turn] it into the forms of animated TV shows we see today, which was a big step at the time."

Astro Boy is considered an overnight sensation and it "showed that anime could actually work as a weekly anime TV show and still pull in a big anime audience." It was produced at a loss of ¥1 million to ¥2 million per episode, but the gamble built a national hit and introduced foreign audiences to Japanese animation.

Media Mix
1960s – 1970s

Anime Connects to Toys, Cards & Media

As TV anime became more common, companies began realizing that animated characters could be sold beyond the screen. Producers in the 1960s were approached by toy and card manufacturers who wanted to license anime products. A manga character could become a toy, card, poster — something physical that could be held.

By the 1970s, anime was part of a larger "media ecology" which involved TV viewing, cinema, comic reading, and fandoms. The 1970s are "frequently seen as laying the framework for the coming decade." Space Battleship Yamato (1974–1977) proved devoted fans would pay for films, magazines, records, and merchandise. Anime magazines like Animage created anticipation and organized fan culture. VHS and home video allowed anime to move beyond scheduled TV times.

OVAs & Global Franchises
1983 – 1988

OVAs Create a Direct Market & Anime Goes Global

In 1983, Dallos became the earliest Original Video Animation release — "the first direct-to-video anime release." OVAs were anime made for home video rather than TV. This allowed for a broad platform of interests and people to have consistent access to anime. The OVAs had more action, more emotion, and deeper stories to tell.

The 1980s helped anime become more recognizable internationally. "Anime in the '80s was nothing short of a revelation. Gone were the simple formulaic cartoons of the past decade. In their place, the entertainment industry was offering complex narratives, breathtaking visuals, and characters that were simply too incredible not to fall in love with." Key franchises that helped fully globalize anime were Mobile Suit Gundam, Dragon Ball, Saint Seiya, and Captain Tsubasa.

Changes International Reputation
1988

Akira Changes International Reputation

When Akira aired, it became an international film that demonstrated that Japanese animation could be cinematic, passionate, political, and futuristic. Anime was no longer seen as children's cartoons but rather a market for science fiction fans, film enthusiasts, and adult animation audiences.

Akira reinforced the idea that manga provides complex storytelling that appeals to modern audiences. Akira also expanded the interest in anime by appealing to broader audiences. The film has an iconic scene — the Akira Slide — with many series having done homages to it, demonstrating its lasting cultural impact.

Sailor Moon, Dragon Ball Z & TV Globalization

Anime became visible on international television. "The spread of VHS tapes, cable television, and fan sub communities expanded the accessibility of anime across Europe, Latin America and North America even when official distribution was limited."

Dragon Ball Z reached huge audiences because of the action based storyline, and Sailor Moon expanded anime's reach among younger female audiences. Dubbing of stories and translating made anime easier for audiences to consume. This continued to add to the global scale of anime.

Pokemon

Pokemon was a global breakthrough for anime because it showed how one idea could move through games, cards, television, toys, movies, and merchandise at the same time.

By 1999, Pokemon: The First Movie generated $163 million worldwide on top of the $150 billion the franchise had already produced, proving anime films could work in Western cinemas.

The Fan Network
2000s

Fan Subbing

Before anime became as dominant as it is, international fans watched anime through fan-subtitled copies online. "In Italy, fansubbing communities like Itasa and Subspedia began to form, creating dedicated online platforms for sharing translations of television series, anime, and films. Their members were not professional translators, but language enthusiasts driven by passion, curiosity, and a shared sense of community."

The practice of fansubbing spread globally, reaching countries and audiences where there was no legal distribution of anime. Early video-sharing platforms helped anime reach audiences that had no access to it otherwise.

The Streaming Revolution
2010s

Streaming

Streaming changed how anime was distributed. Crunchyroll, Netflix, Hulu, and other platforms made anime available on demand and turned international fan demand into a measurable global market.

The statistics show how large that shift became: Netflix anime viewing passed 1 billion views in 2024, more than 50% of Netflix members watch anime, and catalog titles still produce 80% of anime hours viewed.

Licensing

As anime became popular, licensing became one of the industry's main profit engines. Streaming rights, manga translation, games, toys, apparel, theme parks, and collectibles now compete around the same intellectual property.

The statistics PDF shows why this model matters: merchandising represents 31% to 48.4% of the global anime market, the 2024 merchandising segment reached about $10.22 billion, and manga is projected to reach $43.85 billion by 2033.

Cultural Diplomacy
2020s

Soft Power & Anime

Anime became important for Japan's cultural image by turning fictional worlds into real travel, merchandise, and diplomatic attention. This soft power now has measurable economic effects.

In 2024, 13.1% of international visitors to Japan traveled explicitly to visit movie and anime-related locations, contributing to the country's record $53.3 billion in inbound tourism spending.

Global Market

Market research confirms that anime has become an expanding global industry. In 2024, the total global anime market reached 3.84 trillion yen, marking a 14.8% year-over-year increase.

That scale is being produced by a strained labor base: Japan produced 310 new anime series in 2024 with a workforce of fewer than 6,000 trained animators.
